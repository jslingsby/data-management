5.1 library(osfr) for interacting with the Open Science Framework (OSF) from R

library(osfr) is an R package that allows you to interact with OSF directly from R. OSF is a free and open source project management repository designed to support researchers across their entire project lifecycle. The service includes unlimited cloud storage and file version history, providing a centralized location for all your research materials that can be kept private, shared with select collaborators, or made publicly available with citable DOIs. You can use library(osfr) to explore publicly accessible projects and download the associated files — all you need to get started is the project’s URL or GUID (global unique identifier). library(osfr) can also be used for project management by creating projects, add sub-components or directories, and uploading files.

Contributor: Jasper

5.2 Samuel & König-Ries (2021) “Understanding experiments and research practices for reproducibility: An exploratory study. PeerJ”

This study discusses how even though over 90% of researchers agree that there is a reproducibility crisis, there is still a lack of effort to increase reproducibility. This study identifies some of the barriers to this as poor documentation, a lack of shared data and code, as well as insufficient openness around methods.Another contributing factor is also institutional culture and incentives surrounding research.

https://peerj.com/articles/11140/

Contributor: Luke Reed

5.3 (Zaiko et al., 2022) Towards reproducible metabarcoding data: Lessons from an international cross-laboratory experiment.

High-throughput sequencing (HTS) techniques such as DNA meta-barcoding are advancing rapidly. These techniques are proving to be invaluable in biology and ecology, allowing scientists to rapidly and accurately detect species across a wide range of applications. These techniques involve many complex steps and materials, which if not recorded properly can seriously impede the creation of reproducible results. This study investigated the factors that introduce variability into HTS based studies through an “international cross-laboratory experiment”. Through sending off a similar set of samples, standardizing materials such as primers and giving each laboratory the same instructions for DNA sequencing, the study was able to identify specific areas of variability. Although this paper is quite technical and jargon heavy, if read slowly and with intention it provides valuable insights into ensuring your DNA meta-barcoding work is reproducible. I am using DNA meta-barcoding in my honours project and this paper has given me some important things to keep in mind.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1755-0998.13485

Contributor: Joshua Dwyer-Thiem

5.4 ReproducibiliTEA

ReproducibiliTea is a global grassroots initiative that supports early career researchers in building communities around open and reproducible research. It was founded in 2018 at the University of Oxford, ad it provides a simple framework for setting up journal clubs that focus on improving research practices, transparency, and data sharing. The clubs are free, therefore lowering barriers for students and early career researchers to engage in conversations about reproducibility and the replication crisis. https://reproducibilitea.org/

5.5 5.42 Fidler et al. (2017) “Metaresearch for Evaluating Reproducibility in Ecology and Evolution”

This paper focuses on the issues of reproducibility in ecology and evolution by focusing on metaresearch, which refers to research that evaluates research practices. The paper distinguishes between different forms of reproducibility, including direct re-analysis, where the same analysis is repeated using the original data and code; direct computational reproducibility; conceptual replication, which tests the same hypothesis using different methods; and conceptual re-analysis, which involves analysing the same dataset using alternative but justified approaches. The authors explain that direct replications assess reliability, while conceptual replications evaluate whether findings and theories are generalisable. The paper argues that irreproducibility in ecology is often not just due to fraud, but rather poor study design, inappropriate statistical methods, incomplete reporting of results, publication bias, and questionable research practices such as p-hacking and HARKing. The research culture which demands publication as the only path to academic success together with restricted research data and code sharing incentives creates more problems from this situation. The authors suggest that systematic journal surveys could be used to assess statistical reporting and transparency, and that automated tools such as statcheck may help detect inconsistencies. They conclude that a significant proportion of research funding may support irreproducible work, which has both financial and environmental implications.

Link: https://doi.org/10.1093/biosci/biw159

Contributor:Reece Sinn Frantz

5.6 Lee and Hange (2020) “Reproducibility in science: important or incremental?”

This article stresses the importance of reproducibility in the field of genomic epidemiology. Both analytical and laboratory techniques in this field are rapidly evolving. Additionally analysis techniques are not standardised across different groups. The article proposes older studies be revisited and updated periodically. They argue this is important not only for research but also for public health. The article highlights that journals often dismiss reproducibility studies for lacking novelty and only being of incremental benefit. The authors of the article argue that pushing for novel studies contributes to the issue by not encouraging researchers to revisit their work and the works of others. They believe journals should be supporting the publication of reproducibility studies.

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666524720300288

Contributor: Ella

5.7 GitHub Enables Collaborative and Reproducible Laboratory Research

The article emphasizes that reproducible research is essential for verifying, building upon, and trusting scientific findings, ensuring that results are reliable and can be extended by others. Traditional laboratory practices, which rely on scattered notebooks, emails, and unstructured files, often hinder reproducibility and collaboration. The authors discuss how using tools like GitHub allows researchers to document all aspects of their work, from experimental design to data collection, analysis, and manuscript preparation, in a single, organized platform. This integrated approach improves transparency, efficiency, and communication within research teams. By combining version control, project tracking, and comprehensive documentation, teams can monitor changes over time, preserve computational environments, reduce errors, and make results easier to verify.

Although GitHub has some limitations, such as not being optimized for very large datasets, the authors argue that it provides a flexible and scalable framework for reproducible workflows. The principles extend beyond purely computational research and are relevant to biological and ecological studies, particularly those involving complex datasets or multi-institution collaborations. Importantly, reproducibility is presented not merely as publishing final results but as embedding transparent, systematic practices throughout the research process, benefiting individual scientists and the broader scientific community by fostering reliable and collaborative science.

Link to the article: https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3003029

Contributor: Sindiswa

5.8 Lortie et al. (2022) “Tabular strategies for metadata in ecology, evolution, and the environmental sciences”

Lortie et al. highlight the importance of metadata and propose a standardised way of documenting ecological metadata in a table format. This makes it easier to compare metadata between datasets, promotes reproducibility and streamlines workflow when working on projects. Like how datasets can be organised and standardised as “tidy tables” in R using tidyverse packages, the same can be done for metadata. The paper gives examples on how to structure and organise metadata and shows step by step how to do this. It also gives details on tools that can assist you in formatting your metadata.

https://doi: 10.1002/ece3.9245

Contributor: Rebecca Peacocke

5.9 Reproducibility: The science communities’ ticking timebomb. Can we still trust published research?

“If we don’t know how you got these results, how are we supposed to check they’re right?”

In this blog post, Miyako Rogers delves into the “reproducibility crisis”, some possible fixes, and the value of negative results. She also considers the perspective of a researcher who does not believe there is a reproducibility crisis.

While immense pressure to publish appears to be the primary contributor, Rogers suggests that there are several issues driving the crisis, including “p-hacking” (selectively analysing data to bolster non-significant results); HARKing (hypothesising after the results are known); lack of detail, transparency and accessibility; and a growing movement against using p-values to determine significance.

Alexander Bird, a philosopher from King’s College London, argues that “issues with the scientific process” and the base rate fallacy (i.e. giving weight to specific information over statistics) are to blame for irreproducibility, and that the “crisis” is an exaggeration. In her conclusion, Rogers emphasises the need for concrete, thorough and rigorous science.

Link: https://frontlinegenomics.com/reproducibility-the-science-communities-ticking-timebomb-can-we-still-trust-published-research/

Contributor: Grethen

5.10 TEDx Talk: Dan Hammer – Why make environmental data accessible?

Dan Hammer’s talk frames environmental protection as a data problem: we can’t manage ecosystems well if we can’t see change clearly, consistently, and at scale. He shows how modern data streams (especially satellite imagery) can turn the planet into something we can monitor almost continuously, revealing patterns like forest loss that field surveys alone can’t capture everywhere. Through his work linked to Global Forest Watch, he believes the key shift is from raw observations to usable data products. How standardised, validated outputs (such as maps or alerts) can help decision-makers act quickly. In an ecology data-management context, the lesson is that impact depends on more than analysis; it depends on good data stewardship. Clear metadata and methods, repeatable processing pipelines, transparent uncertainty/limitations, and open access allow others to verify, reuse, and build on the work. In short, strong data management makes ecological science accountable and decision-relevant, turning monitoring into timely action rather than static reports.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ph439t-kTIE

5.14 Ivimey-Cook et al. (2025) “From policy to practice: progress towards data- and code-sharing in ecology and evolution”

Code and data sharing are a key component in creating reproducible research. However, in ecology and evolution journals, compliance with policy regarding code and data sharing is poor. This paper investigated data and code sharing policies and compliance for 275 journals in ecology and evolution. The study found that only 22.5% encouraged data sharing and only 38.2% made data sharing mandatory. Similarly, only 26.6% and 26.9% had encouraged and mandatory code sharing, respectively. This paper also provides seven recommendations for journals on how to improve data and code sharing policies and compliance. Recommendations included: policies must be findable and clear, code sharing must be mandatory, policies must be applicable to all types of data, and journals should have data editors to assess data and code quality. This paper is interesting as it looks at the role of policy in promoting reproducible research and how journals can more effectively create and uphold policies.

https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2025.1394

Contributor: Hannah

5.15 James H. Stagge et al. (2019): “Assessing data availability and research reproducibility in hydrology and water resources”

This study evaluated the transparency and reproducibility of research within the hydrology and water resource sciences. As these studies are essential to properly support water management, drought and flood forecasting, and climate adaptation, the reliability of this type of research is absolutely vital. The authors review published articles do determine if they contain the main components required to reproduce the results, including raw data, metadata, the code, and other methodlogies, and whether these components were publically available. It was revealed that many studies said that their data was public, however very few were truly reproducible. Datasets weren’t accessible, lacked enough documentation to allow reuse, and code sharing was almost unheard of. Only 6-8% of the 500 studies reviewed were provided accessible data, with over 70% not proividing a direct link to their raw datasets. Less than 5% included their model code, and multiple papers stated data was available ‘upon request’, only to never be provided.

https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2019.30

Contributor: Carys

5.16 Multi-Stakeholder Research Data Management Training as a Tool to Improve the Quality, Integrity, Reliability and Reproducibility of Research

This paper focuses on something very important for data management and reproducible research: it evaluates a training program designed to enhance research data management (RDM) competencies among doctoral students and postdoctoral researchers. The program was quite interesting because it was collaborative, bringing together multiple stakeholders. It was also at a generic level, which means it was designed not to be overly specific to one discipline but rather relevant across a range of disciplines. Their finding was essentially that training can improve understanding of RDM practices. Feedback and competency ratings from participants in the program indicated that “key RDM practices, such as data planning, documentation, and organization, were better understood after the training.”

https://liberquarterly.eu/article/view/11726/13897

5.17 Lisa DeBruine and Dale Barr (2021) “Data Skills for Reproducible Research”

Producing reproducible research requires learning certain skills and tools which allow for efficiently managing your data and record details of your analysis. DeBruine and Barr first show how to create a project in R Studio and the benefits for your reproducible workflow in doing so as you have a clear filing system which makes reading in data easy, for example. Secondly, an introductory tutorial on R Markdown is covered including how to embed exploratory visual analyses, such as figures, and tables, and preliminary analysis results in your R Markdown script. Finally, citing statistical packages is covered, which is essential in making the process of your data analysis more transparent and easier to replicate.

5.18 Reproducibility trial: 246 biologists get different results from same data sets

In this article by Anil Oza, they speak on a paper doing research on the reproducability of ecological data. The paper (which is under peer review) showed that 246 different scientist got widely different results on the same research question and data set. This gives rise to their question of what can be trusted as a true and proper analysis with a correct result. Most of it boils down to not having a structure in place of how to analyse the data provided and different researchers having their own way to analyse the data. Having power-analyses to find the correct sample sizes and other standardised ways of reviewing the data that is not part of our field necessarily, like robustness tests used in economics, is a part of their solution. This article and paper further cements the importance of reproducability and its practices.

Link to article: https://www-nature-com.ezproxy.uct.ac.za/articles/d41586-023-03177-1

5.20 British Ecological Society (2014) “A Guide to Data Management in Ecology and Evolution”

The Guide to Data Management in Ecology and Evolution is a comprehensive resource for early researchers and focuses on the importance of data management in producing high-quality, easily accessible, and reusable research data. It explains what data and data management is and why it is important as well as provides advice and examples of good data management practices. It covers the various stages of the data lifecycle, including planning, creating, processing, documenting, preserving, sharing, and reusing data. The guide emphasizes the growing importance of collaborative and interdisciplinary research, highlighting the British Ecological Society’s (BES) efforts in promoting data archiving and accessibility. It also addresses challenges and best practices in data collection, digitization, processing, documentation, preservation, and sharing of data. The guide also addresses ethical aspects, such as intellectual property rights, licenses, and permissions related to the reuse of data. It provides practical advice on file naming, assuring high-quality data, processing the data, version control, documenting the data, creating metadata, using non-generic file formats, and organizing data effectively.

See: https://www.britishecologicalsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/Publ_Data-Management-Booklet.pdf

Contributor: Melissa Malan

5.21 The British Ecological Society (2017) “A Guide to Reproducible Code in Ecology and Evolution”

The guide, created by the British Ecological society, is a fully comprehensive practical guide on achieving reproducibility in ecological and evolutionary research. The guide covers a wide range of topics, from the initial steps needed to create a project workflow, to organising projects, programming, version control and report writing. This guide makes use of clear and easy to follow instructions, “do’s and don’t’s” and examples of code, naming files or documents, citations etc. These recommendations and tools are tailored to specific challenges and contexts of data analysis and report writing within ecological and evolutionary research. Where necessary, clear and concise definitions are provided for the reader. These help to unpack the foreign and often confusing terms that come with learning new software like Git. This guide is particularly helpful in explaining how different packages can be beneficial in streamlining the entire report writing process. For example, finely controlling how figures are generated and placed within your report using the package “knitr”.

See https://www.britishecologicalsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/guide-to-reproducible-code.pdf for more.

Contributor: Robyn

5.22 Six factors affecting reproducibility in life science research and how to handle them

https://www.nature.com/articles/d42473-019-00004-y

The advertisement feature gives a good overview of the topic of reproducibility. It starts by outlining what reproducibility is, and how it has been problematic in science since the majority of published papers are currently not reproducible. The article then explains several factors, that cause the lack of reproducibility. These include the inaccessibility of methodological details, the use of misidentified of contaminated samples in microbiology, the difficulty many researchers have with managing complex datasets, bad experimental designs and research practices, cognitive bias, and the pressure to publish novel and extraordinary findings. The article then goes on to recommend ways that help in producing reproducible research, and outlines the work of several initiatives, that address and support the issues in research reproducibility.

Contributor: Svenja

5.23 Braga et al. (2023) “Not just for programmers: How GitHub can accelerate collaborative and reproducible research in ecology and evolution. Methods in Ecology and Evolution”

https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.14108

This article provides practical guidance for researchers in the field Ecology and Evolutionary biology on how to use GitHub. In the article, GitHub is described as “the most used web-based platform which has features for more collaborative, transparent, and reproducible research”. Furthermore, the article outlines features from low to high technical difficulties one might experience with using GitHub including, code and coding management, writing a manuscript, conducting peer review, using automated and continuous integration to streamline analyses, which allows for early detection and correction of errors, potentially improving confidence in scientific development. In addition, the article provides definitions for basic concepts of Git (such as commit, push, pull, fork, repository etc), for users who are less familiar with software terms. The article also provides relevant information like how GitHub limits committed file sizes to 100 megabytes, meaning that a person may require external file storage alternatives. Furthermore, how long-term management of laboratory materials tool such as notebooks etc can also be done within GitHub. Finally, how GitHub also allows for centralized communication for all project-related conversation and planning for a team, rather than emailing, which makes it easier to keep track of progress throughout the lifespan of a project.

Open Science Framework repository (accessible at https://osf.io/bypfm/; Braga et al., 2023) and GitHub repository (accessible at https://github.com/SORTEE-Github-Hackathon/manuscript) for this study.

Contributor: Ongezile Mpehle

5.24 Grames, E.M. and Elphick, C.S., 2020. Use of study design principles would increase the reproducibility of reviews in conservation biology. Biological Conservation

https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.108385

This scientific article highlights the dangers of biological reviews if not conducted in a reproducible way. Biological reviews are very useful when researching to gain an overview of the topic and the trends seen worldwide. However, these reviews need to be taken with a pinch of salt as one should conduct a thorough assessment of the methods before taking the review into account. Grames and Elphick (2020) suggest five steps to conduct a review to have the same reproducible standard that experiments and field studies should have. The first step is “searching for studies” and most reviews they found do not explain how they found the studies used in the review. This highlights the potential for selection bias based on familiarity, access, and time when the search was done. Trying to repeat a review without the full methods of how the search was conducted would be impossible. The second step is “selecting studies for inclusion” as reviews must define their selection criteria. Without defining these criteria clearly, selection bias can occur based on familiarity or choosing studies that agree with the result that the reviewer wishes to achieve. The third step is “critically appraising the quality of included studies” as different studies are carried out to a different standard and thus have varying strength of evidence. The fourth step is “coding studies and extracting data”. Variables need to be defined, just like in regular studies. The fifth step is “synthesizing results” and many reviews do not explain how the results are synthesized leading these reviews to be irreproducible. This paper ends off by emphasizing the need for biological reviews to have a standardised format to ensure reproducibility.

I chose this paper as I have read and used many reviews in projects and research tasks. It makes me question their methods and how one must be careful when using a review paper. They are still valuable but need to be well scrutinised.

5.55 From policy to practice: progress towards data- and code-sharing in ecology and evolution (Ivimey-Cook et al., 2025)

Scientific journals play a crucial role in facilitating the reproducibility of research through data and code sharing policies. However, the clarity, stringency and the timing of journal sharing policies vary across ecology and evolution journals. These three metrics were tested across 275 journals by Ivimey-Cook et al.’s 2025 paper. Only 38.2% of the investigated journals enforced data sharing, and only 26.9% mandated code sharing, both of which were mainly used to help the peer review process during the publication process. The authors acknowledge the importance of reproducibility for the credibility of science and the paper goes on to explain seven key pieces of advice for ecology and evolution journals to improve their mandates on data and code sharing including monitoring compliance, employing data editors, and aligning policies. Implementing data and code sharing policies by journals will increase the overall accessibility of data and code to ultimately improve the reproducibility of research.

5.56 DataFed: Towards Reproducible Research via Federated Data Management

This paper emphasises the need for an efficient scientific data management system (SDMS) given the current explosion in data volumes and the need to share data. With research becoming more multidisciplinary and globalized, the need to publish your data sets to support research findings is becoming increasingly important. DataFed is a lightweight SDMS that allows scientists to organize, find and move data that is stored all over the globe. It connects different storage systems together and is user-friendly. It improves on other SDMS by using fast, scalable tools for managing users and transferring large datasets.
